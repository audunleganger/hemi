Three directions for Hemi

Each direction shows the same moment on a phone and on a 16:10 PC screen: Monday 19 October, 09:00. You woke up with an attack at 06:40, took Imigran nasal spray at 06:45, and the two-hour rating is now due. All the numbers are example data. The pain-side icon is the head seen from behind, so the filled half is your own left.

A

Familiar

Larder's sibling. It uses the same system font, cards, bottom tab bar and semantic colors, with the blue accent swapped for indigo. The front page stacks the ongoing attack, the pending rating and the limits. On a PC it becomes a sidebar with a three-column dashboard.

Good

  • Feels like the same family as Larder, and can reuse its components and tokens.
  • Cheapest to build and to keep consistent.
  • Everything is visible on one screen.

Less good

  • Busy and bright in the middle of an attack. Only the night theme softens it.
  • Severity uses the warning colors (yellow, orange, red), which read as alarms.

B

Dim

Built for the attack itself. It's dark first, with warm amber instead of blue light and a font designed for legibility. While an attack is going on, the phone shows only four huge severity buttons and your usual medication. Tapping a number records a reading. Everything else is a tap away in the menu. On a PC the controls sit in a left panel, with the attack's timeline and overview to the right.

Good

  • Usable in a dark room with a pounding head: a reading or a dose is one tap.
  • Little text, large targets, no white areas.
  • Atkinson Hyperlegible was made for readers with low vision, which helps when your eyes hurt.

Less good

  • History and statistics feel like a second app behind the menu.
  • Needs a proper light version too, which is more design work.
  • Sparse on a wide screen between attacks.

C

Diary

The calendar is the home screen. On a phone the month sits at the top and the selected day's timeline below it: readings, doses and ratings in order. A floating Record button does the logging. On a PC a full-year strip runs across the top, with the month, the day and the limits underneath. Severity uses one plum scale, from pale to deep, instead of alarm colors.

Good

  • Patterns are visible at a glance: clusters, gaps, medication days.
  • Makes the most of a wide screen, and is good to bring to a doctor's appointment.
  • Calm colors. Schibsted Grotesk is a Norwegian typeface, made for the Schibsted newspapers.

Less good

  • Logging during an attack goes through the Record button: two taps instead of one.
  • The ongoing attack shares the screen with the calendar.

Picking one

You don't have to take a direction whole. The parts combine well, for example C as the everyday app with B's screen taking over while an attack is ongoing.

Things worth deciding along with the direction: the severity colors (alarm colors as in A, amber as in B, or one calm scale as in C), whether the app switches to the dim look on its own during an attack, and phone navigation (tab bar, or a floating Record button).
